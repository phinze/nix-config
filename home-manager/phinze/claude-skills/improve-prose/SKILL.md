# Improve prose

Help the reader get the intended point quickly and correctly. Preserve the
author's facts, level of certainty, commitments, and recognizable voice. Clear
prose can still be warm, conversational, and a little weird.

This is not a controlled-language compliance check. Do not enforce a restricted
vocabulary, fixed sentence length, or ban on contractions. Optimize for the
reader, not an AI-detector score or a generic idea of what sounds human.

## Find the point

Infer the audience, purpose, and desired response from the request and source
material. Ask only when a missing answer would materially change the result.

For a new draft, state the point before writing the supporting detail. For a
revision, first identify what each passage is trying to do. Reorganize around
that purpose instead of polishing the existing order sentence by sentence.

If the user supplies a writing sample, treat it as the strongest evidence about
voice. Notice its rhythm, word choice, punctuation, formality, humor, and
deliberate quirks. Preserve those traits unless they obstruct the meaning. Do
not inject casualness, opinion, or first-person language where it does not fit.

## Make it easy to follow

- Lead with the outcome, request, decision, or other information the reader
  came for. Add context in the order needed to understand it.
- Give each sentence one main idea and each paragraph one job. Use transitions
  when the relationship between ideas is not already clear.
- Name the actor when it matters. Prefer direct, specific verbs to passive
  constructions and abstract noun phrases.
- Keep the subject, main verb, and object close enough that the action is easy
  to find. Put a modifier next to the word or phrase it modifies.
- Prefer familiar, precise words. Define necessary jargon for the actual
  audience, and use the same term for the same idea throughout.
- Say whether an action is required, recommended, optional, or merely possible.
  Do not hide that distinction behind an ambiguous *should*.
- Do not call a task simple, easy, obvious, or quick. Explain the action or omit
  the judgment.
- Put a condition before the instruction it governs. In procedures, keep each
  step focused on one action unless the actions must happen together.
- Use parallel structure for genuinely comparable ideas. Do not force unrelated
  sections into identical shapes or pad a list to make it feel complete.
- Use a list when the material is genuinely list-shaped. Do not turn an
  explanation into bullets just to make it look scannable.

## Avoid the chatbot shape

Treat these as patterns to inspect, not forbidden words or constructions. One
instance can be natural; several stacked together usually deserve a rewrite.

- Inflated significance, promotional adjectives, or claims that a routine fact
  marks a broader shift without evidence.
- An abstract *-ing* tail that pretends to explain why the preceding fact
  matters without adding information.
- Predictable triads, repeated section templates, uniform sentence rhythm, or a
  tidy takeaway after every point.
- A staged opener, announcement of the next point, or first sentence that only
  repeats its heading.
- An objection, contrast, or rejected alternative that no reader raised and the
  rest of the text does not use.
- A generic optimistic conclusion after the useful content has already ended.

Fix the underlying claim or structure. Do not merely trade a watched word for a
synonym.

## Cut without flattening

Remove throat-clearing, repeated conclusions, empty intensifiers, and phrases
that merely announce what the next sentence will say. Replace wordy action
nouns with verbs when that makes the actor and action clearer.

Treat a long sentence as a reason to inspect its structure, not as an automatic
failure. Do not shorten prose until it becomes choppy, cryptic, or abrupt. Keep
ordinary contractions and natural rhythm.

Never cut a qualification that changes certainty, scope, responsibility, or
risk. Do not add confidence, claims, commitments, or causal explanations that
the source does not support.

When editing a file, change prose only unless the user asks for more. Preserve
code blocks, commands, identifiers, direct quotations, data, frontmatter, and
link targets exactly. Leave passages that are already clear alone instead of
rewriting the whole artifact into one uniform voice.

## Check the result

Read the finished prose as its intended reader. Make sure the point arrives
early, references are unambiguous, terminology stays consistent, and every
paragraph earns its place. Read awkward passages aloud when cadence or sentence
structure still feels suspect.

For a longer argument, ask what new fact, claim, or turn each paragraph adds.
Cut a paragraph that only restates the premise. If body paragraphs can trade
places without changing the logic, either present them as an explicit list or
build a through-line that makes their order matter.

Compare a revision with its source. Make sure every fact, number, date,
citation, qualification, and commitment survives, and that the rewrite invents
none.

## Return the result

Return the finished prose in the form the user requested. Keep editing
commentary separate and brief. When another workflow uses this skill as one
step, return only the artifact it needs.
